Friends often ask me for some advice on starting or restarting running. Here is
what I tell them.

1. **Set a goal ~12-16 weeks in the future.** The best way is to register for a
   race. Having a race on the calendar always provides me motivation: it's a
   date by which I need to complete the training program and test my fitness. If
   in New York City, I highly recommend finding an [NYRR
   race](https://www.nyrr.org/run/race-calendar) of any distance and making a
   goal even of just completing it. You can either start smart by registering
   for a friendly 5km (3.1 mi) or maybe even do something dumb like register for
   [an ultramarathon or Ironman](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vWU5O7cK7aI).
   (Don’t actually do those last suggestions.)

    Sixteen weeks is typically the longest length of most training programs, but
    others are 12 or 14 weeks. This leads me to…
    
2. **Start on a prescribed training program** that builds toward the goal.
   Using a training plan provided me with extra motivation: I really like being
   able to see what I need to do *today* to get to my goal in a few weeks time.
   These programs always supply a calendar and maybe an app that tells you what
   to do today and what to look forward to I have mostly used [Greg McMillan's
   plans](https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/training-plans/) (his general marathon
   and Boston Marathon-specific training programs helped me set PRs). I
   personally had started with [Hal Higdons's
   plans](https://www.halhigdon.com/training/), which are also good, at least
   for the novice and intermediate ones. [Couch to 5k
   (C25K)](http://www.c25k.com/) is a popular, free option too!

    McMillan, Higdon, and others have books that describe their methodologies,
    but are mostly not needed when just starting out. I liked [McMillan's "You
    (Only
    Faster)"](https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/product/you-only-faster-by-greg-mcmillan/);
    it is very approachable and pretty well written.
    
3. **Be consistent.** Any plan is likely good as long as you stick to it. Even
   short of engaging in a formal plan, my recommendation is to just *be
   consistent*. Pick a handful of days to go running — and it can be as short of
   a run as you want — and commit to going under most circumstances. Even if it
   is raining. Even if it’s only for 20 minutes. Et cetera.

   Getting into running is hard. It gets more fun and enjoyable the more you do
   it. (I’m constantly reminded of the opening of [an essay by Brendan
   Leonard](https://semi-rad.com/2017/01/i-hate-running/): “I hate running,
   three to four times a week if I have time. I hated it yesterday for a little
   over an hour… But you should totally try it.” But see [his follow-up
   essay](https://semi-rad.com/2025/04/update-i-dont-actually-hate-running-now/)
   as well.)

   For me, running is meditation in the moment and creates a high that lasts my
   entire day. But it only works if I get past the hurdle of starting (whether
   it be after some time off due to illness, or on a cold, dark morning) and do
   it consistently.

4. **Listen to your body, start low and slow.** Don't be a slave to the plan:
   staying healthy is paramount. Running is harsh on the body: the peak forces
   experienced while running can be up to 3x your body weight. Getting your body
   adapted to these forces and stresses takes time.  Start at a low mileage and
   a slow pace. Build up over time: a typical rule of thumb is to increase next
   week’s mileage by no more than 10% of the previous week’s. Run at a
   conversational pace: one that you would feel comfortable having a chat with
   someone next to you with minimal interruption from heavy breathing. This easy
   pace will get faster over time; eventually, the prescribed training plan will
   give instructions on when and how to run at higher effort.

5. **Be prepared to deal with setbacks.** The saying is that at least 50% of
   runners get hurt every year. If you get hurt (something is sore or
   sensitive) or injured (something is broken, torn, or inoperative), you might
   be able to run through it. You might not. Even professional runners have a
   hard time figuring this out. Take time off if you need it, but it’s better
   to work around the injury however you can and still use the allotted time
   for exercise: use the elliptical, do some strength training, go swimming,
   get on a bike, etc. Do what you can with what you have, but continue to be
   consistent in doing *something*. Start running again when you can.

5. **Find a specialty running shoe store**. One way to stave off injury is
   having the right shoes. When I was in New York, I liked the [Fleet Feet
   location in the Time Warner Center](https://goo.gl/maps/rZidtReDGbFNJKpd9).
   I've generally had a good experience there. I even recently took advantage of
   Fleet Feet's no-questions-asked return policy for a shoe that started to fall
   apart after a couple of dozen miles. They also give decent coupons and
   discounts if you sign up for an account.

   Ask them for advice. Run on the treadmill a little bit. Finding what you like
   will be a little bit of trial-and-error, both at the store and for years.

6. **Use running as a tool to make friends and network**. Shared suffering is a
   key way to create meaningful relationships as an adult and having a running
   partner will add some social pressure to be consistent. They can even become
   personal and professional contacts.

   For example, if your company has a running group, go join them. In my case,
   almost every Google office has a running club with a regular schedule. It's a
   great way to meet fellow Googlers from disparate teams. It's an easy-paced
   run and there is likely someone who will run with you. And, when visiting a
   remote office, drop in on their runs.

   Otherwise, look for a local club with regular runs. There are a [huge number
   of clubs](https://www.nyrr.org/getinvolved/club) available in NYC; I didn't
   run in any of them, mostly out of laziness in trying to find one I liked and
   already having pre-existing training partners, but if I were there now, I’d
   definitely join one.

   Or, ask around in your existing peer group.

7. **Turn your commute into a run**. One thing I really enjoyed for its time
   efficiency was running to work. I had to get to work somehow anyway and I
   might as well run, since Google generously provides showers and towels.
   Running with a backpack — especially one designed for running, and even
   filled with a laptop and a change of clothes — is really not bad.

8. **Create a [Strava](https://www.strava.com/) account**. It’s the only social
   network that I have an account on and actively use. Not only do I get kudos
   and words of encouragement, but I can share successes and failures (in a
   specific domain) with my friends and also get motivated by their own
   endeavors (a positive take on FOMO). It's also useful just to track weekly
   mileage and progress; I end up using the subscriber-only training log a lot.

10. **Get a running-specific watch**. By wearing the watch every day, it was a
    constant reminder to me that I should make use of it and go out for a run. I
    started with the equivalent of the Garmin Forerunner 265 (but the 65 would
    probably have been fine too).  I now have a Garmin Forerunner 955, purchased
    chiefly for its ability to provide turn-by-turn directions, which is
    especially valuable when running while traveling.

11. **Watch some inspiring videos**. There is no shortage of YouTube running
    content that will make you want to go out for a run now or stick to the plan
    and race in a few weeks.

    I don’t have great recommendations for beginner runner channels; the ones I
    subscribe to are mostly faster runners that I can look up to for my own
    training. That said, I really like: [Eric
    Floberg](https://www.youtube.com/@flobergruns), [Allie
    Ostrander](https://www.youtube.com/@allie_ostrander), [The Athlete
    Special](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCTVjyeSlm3sR3N1fx-NI-Kw), [Ran to
    Japan](https://www.youtube.com/@RanToJapan),
    [Kofuzi](https://www.youtube.com/kofuzi), [Adam
    Wood](https://www.youtube.com/@AdamWoodRuns), and [Mica Rivera
    Wood](https://www.youtube.com/@micawood).
    
    Some of my favorite individual videos are: 
    
    1. [“Sisyphus and the Impossible
    Dream”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9IiTdSnmS7E)
    2. [“I Worked Out Like David Goggins for 100 Days”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vWU5O7cK7aI&list=FLVIzgw4unCVB-yt0QGW8r8g&index=11)
    3. [“Six Star Stories: Yuki Kawauchi”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ij0qbKgJv4s)

    I also really enjoyed the feature-length film *[Brittany Runs a
    Marathon](https://www.amazon.com/gp/video/detail/B0875KZKBQ)*, parts of
    which were filmed at the 2017 NYC Marathon.

Getting started is the hardest part, but the "runner’s high" and the sense of
accomplishment at the finish line are well worth the effort.
